Introduction

Controlling inflation

  • InflationA general and sustained increase in prices.
  • The threatIt reduces households’ purchasing power and makes business decisions harder.
  • The responseThe central bank acts on financing conditions to bring inflation back to its price stability target.

How does monetary policy control inflation, and what are its limitations?

Part I

The central bank’s toolkit

  • The goalLimit money creation, which mainly comes from bank lending.
  • The meansInstruments that act on commercial banks’ liquidity and their ability to lend.
  • Three toolsA. Key interest rates · B. Open market operations · C. Reserve requirements.

Controlling the money supply.

Part I · A

Key interest rates

  • The main leverWhen inflation rises, the central bank raises its interest rates.
  • Costlier creditBanks, households and businesses borrow less.
  • Weaker demandConsumption and investment fall, so firms face less pressure to raise prices.
  • Not immediateThe effects spread gradually through the economy.

Higher rates, less borrowing.

Part I · B

Open market operations

  • Injecting liquidityBuying securities from banks raises their reserves and stimulates credit.
  • Withdrawing liquidityTo fight inflation, the central bank sells securities to banks.
  • The effectBanks pay with their reserves: less liquidity, slower money creation, easier inflationary pressure.

Selling securities drains liquidity.

Part I · C

Reserve requirements

  • The ratioThe share of funds banks must hold with the central bank.
  • Raising itBanks set aside more, lend less, and aggregate demand slows.
  • Lowering itResources are released so banks can lend more and stimulate activity.

A higher ratio means fewer loans.

Part II

The transmission mechanisms

  • Indirect actionMonetary policy acts on inflation through a chain of reactions.
  • Restrictive policyIt gradually affects the whole economy and helps slow the rise in prices.
  • The chainKey rates → cost of credit → demand → prices.

One push, and everything follows.

Part II · A

Higher rates, costlier credit

  • Bank ratesBank interest rates go up.
  • LoansMortgages, consumer loans and business loans cost more.
  • Credit demandDemand for credit declines.
  • SavingSaving becomes more attractive.

Households and firms borrow less, so spending and investment slow.

Part II · B

Lower consumption and investment

  • HouseholdsThey postpone major purchases such as housing or durable goods.
  • BusinessesThey postpone investment projects that are less profitable.
  • DemandConsumption, investment and domestic demand all decline.
  • PricesFirms find it harder to raise prices.

Inflationary pressures ease.

Part II · C

Currency appreciation

  • Capital inflowsHigher rates attract foreign capital seeking better returns.
  • A stronger currencyDemand for the national currency rises and it appreciates.
  • Cheaper importsEnergy, raw materials and some foods cost less.
  • ExampleThe Swiss National Bank (SNB).

Imported inflation falls.

Part III

Challenges, limitations and trade-offs

  • A. Recession riskThe trade-off between growth and inflation.
  • B. Time lagsMonetary policy takes time to work.
  • C. Supply shocksLittle effect on cost-push inflation.

Walking a tightrope.

Part III · A

The risk of recession

  • The dilemmaCurbing inflation means compressing demand. A sharp slowdown weighs on activity and jobs.
  • a. Phillips curveA short-term trade-off between price stability and employment.
  • b. Sacrifice ratioThe cumulative GDP loss for a one-point cut in inflation.
  • c. Other constraintsHysteresis, and Mundell’s impossible trinity in a monetary union.

Growth against inflation.

Part III · B

The time lags of monetary policy

  • a. Recognition and decisionData are published late and revised before the bank can decide.
  • b. Financial transmissionRate changes reach lending rates gradually (fixed or variable loans, bank competition).
  • c. Real economy and pricesSpending adjusts slowly, and firms revise prices according to contracts, inventories and billing.

Patience is part of the policy.

Part III · C

Cost-push inflation (supply shocks)

  • a. Not excess demandPrices rise because production costs rise.
  • b. Risk of stagflationHigh inflation, weak growth and rising unemployment.
  • c. Limits of actionThe bank cannot lower the price of a barrel of oil. It prevents second-round effects; fiscal and structural measures can help.

Rates cannot fix an oil shock.

Conclusion

Price stability

  • An essential toolMonetary policy helps control inflation and contribute to price stability.
  • How it worksThrough key interest rates, control of the money supply and anchoring of expectations.
  • Its limitsIt acts with time lags and must consider the origin of inflation and its effects on activity and employment.

Stable prices on one side, a stable economy on the other.
